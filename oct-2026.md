**St Paul’s Suite - I. Jig (1913)**  
_Gustav Holst (1874 - 1934)_
 
Born Gustavus Theodore von Holst, to a British mother and a Swedish/Latvian/German father, Gustav Holst was a third generation musician, who learned to play piano (which he loved, but neuritis prevented him pursuing it - he described his right as “like a jelly overcharged with electricity”) and violin (which he hated). 
 
Around his 21st birthday, Holst met Ralph Vaughan Williams, who became a lifelong friend and the two would often seek each other’s counsel on their compositions over the years. It was also the bicentenary of Henry Purcell (more on him later), which led to various performances of Dido and Aeneas - something that Holst later noted was an unconscious inspiration of his search for “the (or a) musical idiom of the English language”.

In 1903, Holst decided to abandon orchestral playing and focus on composition. To support his income, he became director at the recently formed St Paul’s Girls’ School in Hammersmith from 1905 onwards. Both pieces were play today were written while he was there.

The St Paul’s Suite was written for the school orchestra to play in 1913 to celebrate the opening of a new music wing, which included a sound-proof room where Holst could work on composition undisturbed. Deemed unfit for military service due to his health, Holst was able to write the Planets during those years - although finally was given a chance to serve in the war in 1918 when the YMCA’s music section needed volunteer teachers. His current teaching positions offered him a year of leave, but the YMCA were wary of his very German name. In September 1918, he formally changed his name to “Gustav Holst”.

 
**Quartet No 1 - II. Andante (1921)**  
_Ernest John Moeran (1894 - 1950)_

Moeran was an Anglo-Irish composer who was strongly influenced by English and Irish folk music, alongside many other composers of the time.

His early music studies were interrupted by war. At 19, during World War I, he enlisted as a motor cycle dispatch - although during his leave breaks, he would collect folk music in Norfolk. Sadly, he received a wound in the head in France in 1917, and the surgery (which may or may not have involved inserting a metal plate into his skull) may have attributed to some of his more erratic and unstable behaviours later in life. His last few years of military duty were served in Ireland, where he engaged with his Irish roots and collected more Irish folk songs. Just before the war ended, he also met Arnold Bax (more on him later) in London, who described Moeran as “charming and as good-looking a young office as one could hope to meet”.

The quartet we play comes from this time, when his main influences were folk music and his love of rural England, and what seems like a more joyous time of his life.

**The Planets suite - IV. Jupiter, the bringer of jollity (1918)**  
_Gustav Holst (1874 - 1934) arr Martelli_

Clifford Bax, the brother of Arnold Bax (more on him later), introduced Holst to astrology on a holiday trip in Spain in 1913. Not only did it lead to his inspiration for The Planets, but Holst would cast his friend’s horoscopes for the rest of his life.

The Planets is an iconic piece of music, now over 100 years old and yet still captures the attention of audiences. The idea for the suite was, as Holst described it, a series of mood pictures that would act as foils to one another, depicting the celestial bodies.

The arrangement we're playing of the middle movement, Jupiter, reduces the large orchestra down to a simple string quartet.

Listeners may recognise the inner part of Jupiter having a very hymn-like quality. That section (later named Thaxted after the village Holst lived in) was rearranged to be a hymn, with the words “I vow to thee my country”, which ended up as the school hymn for St Paul’s - as well as a very British song, often played at state funerals and Remembrance Day services.

**Abdelazer (or, The Moor’s Revenge) - II. Rondeau (1695)**  
_Henry Purcell (1659 - 1695)_

The Abdelazar suite is one of incidental music, to accompany a revival of Aphra Behn’s 1676 play “Abdelazer; or, The Moor’s Revenge”, based on a tragedy called Lust’s Dominion. The story talks about a captive Moor living in the Spanish court whose king killed Abdelazer’s father. Abdelazer seeks revenge, becomes the lover of the queen and together plot to poison both the king and do away with the other heirs. As expected from a tragedy, most characters are killed or betrayed, resulting in the young prince ascending the throne while Abdelazer (unrelenting) is killed. Interestingly, Behn’s version minimises the early racial stereotypes prejudicing the Moors - including making the most evil of Abdelazer’s henchmen white.

The Rondeau in particular has become quite popular, appearing in Benjamin Britten’s “The Young Person’s Guide to the Orchestras”, in video games, and in the 2005 production of Pride and Prejudice.

 
**String Quartet No. 1 (1918)**  
_Arnold Bax (1883 - 1953)_

Allegretto semplice  
Lento e molto espressivo  
Rondo (Allegro vivace)  
 
Arnold Bax was born to a prosperous Victorian family (prosperous enough that his father, a barrister, did not practice because he had a private income). Unlike many of the famous British composers of the time who had a strong enthusiasm for folk music (including Vaughan Williams and Holst), Bax sided with Elgar against the trend, going o far as to say “you should make a point of trying every experience once, excepting incest and folk-dancing”.

Although taught primarily the works of Wagner by his composition teacher at the Royal Academy of Music, Bax discovered Strauss and also privately studied the works of Debussy (both generally frowned upon by the largely conservative faculty).

Inspired by the Irish poet Yeats, Bax went to visit the west coast of Ireland in 1902, which inspired his love and appreciation of Celtic music, culture and folklore. An earlier quartet (unnumbered) was written around this time, as Bax sought to conceive of a Celtic idiom. The first numbered quartet that we play today was likely written during World War I (where Bax, much like Holst and Moeran, were not deemed fit to serve - leading to a prolific period of composition). The Irish tunes were noted and commented on, particularly in the last movement, as well as some more Dvorak-esque Slavic tendencies in the first. 
 
**Pasttime with good company (1513)**  
_King Henry VIII (1491 - 1547)_
 
To think a king from the 1500s inspired a musical (Six: divorced, beheaded, died, divorced, beheaded, survived), as well as creating the concept of divorce (when the Pope refused to annul his first marriage, Henry passed the Acts of Supremacy, creating himself as Supreme Head of the Church of England), and perhaps giving us the clearest example of how absolute power corrupts. His later life showed him growing more tyrannical, egotistical, lustful and paranoid.

The start of his life and his reign was a very different picture. This English folk song is perhaps the most famous of King Henry’s creative works, later known as “The King’s Ballad”. It is thought that he wrote the song for Catherine of Aragon, who would become his first wife. Written at a time where the political stability of the kingdom and wealth of the state allowed an era of exuberance and extravagance, the piece praises entertainments and diversions - providing a moral explanation that play and mirth with good company is the right way to live, for idleness leads to all manner of vices. While we don’t condone later behaviour of King Henry VIII, we do agree - and appreciate all of you coming to pass the time with us, with music, drinks and merriment.

### Poems, courtesy of Darsha

*Gustav Holst - St Paul’s Suite, I. Jig*

As the school year began again,   
I found a curious development:   
A new master, a musician  
Come to show us the world  
Not simply one dance at a time,   
(As in past years of playthings and   
Childish sentiments),   
But by learning our way through  
The wide history of music.   
Surprisingly, he felt art was only for the chosen few  
(Aristocracy in art, he’d say) -   
But to find those precious few,  
One must bring art to all,   
Awaiting the bright glow (like fireflies  
In the burgeoning twilight)  
Of those who understood:  
the talented; the true.   
There I sat, day by day,   
Dumbfounded afresh   
By my ignorance of the simplest of things.   
And yet, as the days marched on, I found  
Joy in that simplicity, in the folktunes  
Etched into town squares  
And the beating hearts of all who danced  
At the turning of the seasons;   
Those human melodies  
Profoundly expanded under his mastery.  
I often wondered why he came here  
(This new school) when his heart  
So clearly belonged to composing.   
It was not until later, at the opening  
Of the new music wing,   
And his new offering - a suite  
Just for us - that the penny dropped;  
That even those talented few  
Had need of other means to survive.  
Only by the smile of Fortune herself    
Could fireflies shine as their heart desired,   
Gracing with their precious light  
All who knew where to look.   
Myself, I never saw the glow, never even sought it;   
Deep inside, this new lens on life  
Brought me such delight.   


*E. J. Moeran - String quartet 1, II. Andante*

Transfigured as we find ourselves, adrift  
Without a steady beat or place to be,  
Yet every voice attempting to uplift:  
It seems as if there’s something here to see.   
That liminal sensation slowly folds  
Across the golden, russet swirls of trees,   
A melody imbued with summer’s gold  
Surveys the changing landscape, seeking peace.   
Such mellow, melancholic strains, o’er dells  
Adrift like pleasant amber leaves in fall;  
Their final sweetness brief yet brillig, all  
To swell the gourd, and plump the hazel shells.   
And for such bounty, maybe I shall smile,   
Indulge the timeless; simply stay a while.   


*Gustav Holst - The Planets: IV. Jupiter, the bringer of jollity*

What jolly friends this person has,  
To send them humming all, en masse,  
A tune they didn't think they knew,  
That strikes the core of England true.  

To think this work would otherwise  
Require more than just four guys:  
From all the works and days of hands,  
A full-blown wind and brassy band.   

So let its full regality  
Wash over you, like cups of tea  
Drunk eagerly among your friends  
As off to Jove we now ascend.  


*Henry Purcell - Abdelazer Suite, II. Rondeau*

When faced with time itself, would you declare   
To bow your head, accepting fate’s decree,  
Or statues build? An army mass? Prepare  
A litany of arts so vast, they scream   
“Look on my works, ye mighty, and despair!”?  
Such brazen pride may beggar disbelief,   
Yet listen to this marching music play,  
Of ancient godlike rulers. Time (a thief)  
The antihero prince obscured; instead  
Here stands Purcell, remembered through his songs,   
(His incidental tunes at that). Not bred  
To conquer worlds, nor minded to outlast,   
Yet something in his offerings has let  
His works resound, while others all have passed.   


*Arnold Bax - Quartet 1*

I.

The summer sunlight lingers with  
Such languish in the afternoon,  
Drunk in quite lazily by eager  
Flowers blooming in these hotter months.  
Inhaling, I survey their simple brilliance,  
Serene and safe from winter’s chill  
As off I walk in aimless loops,  
Unhurried and exuberant.

Aimless? Me? Misleading that may be,  
For aim I have: impossibility.  
A task, from legends sung of old.  
A pianist of some high regard  
(At least for me,, considerable sway)  
Had bade me gather her blue roses;  
Flowers fit to flourish for  
A princess and her garden bed.  

II.

That may sound quite familiar,  
The way that childhood memories  
Will grasp your ear and win your smile.  
But every quest is different,  
If only for the feel of things:  
The when, the why, the weathering  
Of where and how we find ourselves.  


The final drops of summer’s sun  
Slipped smoothly down the petals of  
Those roses red and roses white,  
As twilight slowly found me  
Melancholy, wistful, wrapped  
In echoes of my happy days.  

To think that any one of those  
Sweet, fragrant early memories  
Might blossom forth into a song  
Bewitching just enough to seize  
Forever that fair pianist’s gaze.  
I might have let the stillness  
Of that simple sighed reflection  
Bid me sleep in peace for days.  

But somewhere in that wondering,  
I realised that no greater rose  
Could ever grace a lover’s eyes  
Than flowers from the deepest shade  
Of heartfelt love, desired true.  

III.

With haste fly I! Now to return  
To where this story’s tail began.   
As daylight floods the early morn,   
So too do I flit fast as stars  
Flung out across the galaxy;  
My heart’s four quarters overfilled   
With lover’s truest blue resolve  
An offering of life itself.   

You may have felt  my quest was clear:  
Yet though my journey forth a snooze,   
The road’s return is rife with ruse;  
My canter leads us to encounter  
Motley pack of pits and perils,   
Riddles, high seas, elegies,   
Quite fit to frighten witless   
Even Greece’s great tactician.  

Yet just as he steered home at last,   
So too do I triumphant find  
Myself surviving trial by trial  
Until, exuberant again,  
I see familiarity,  
And celebrate the truth of it:  
While here the land seems much the same.  
So much in me for good has changed.  


King Henry VII - pastime with good company 

For even kings of disrepute  
Have moments when their inner flute  
Bids speak the wonders in their heart   
In ever-flowing works of art.   

The passion burgeoning inside  
In dulcet tones, softly confides  
“How do I love thee? Let me count  
The ways in which such feelings mount.”  

Let not such urges ruminate  
In isolated silence. State  
Them proudly as you gather all  
Your closest friends for festive fall.   

For better ‘tis to share one’s life  
With friends; with family; lover; wife.   
Good company is bound to bring  
The happiness that makes you sing.
